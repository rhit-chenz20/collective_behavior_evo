Across theoretical and empirical studies, the same pattern emerges: whether a socially influenced trait evolves faster or slower is not predicted by whether a species is social, but by the specific structure of the interaction. Another field that similarly stresses the structure of social interactions is social learning theory. Rogers' paradox is a foundational result in this literature, stating that unconditional social learning does not raise mean population fitness; instead, the fitness payoff depends on who, when, and what individuals copy, formalized as social learning strategies~\cite{Rogers1988, Laland2004, Rendell2010}. Social learning strategies further decompose the social group by specifying who is copied, offering a more fine-grained perspective than the coarser sociality measures used in the theories discussed above. In this study, we incorporate social learning strategies and explicit genetic architecture into a social interaction model extended from Moore et al.~\cite{Moore1997}. Given the complex, structure-dependent selection patterns observed in empirical systems, we test social populations under different selection modes in an attempt to characterize how different social learning strategies shape population-level evolutionary outcomes.
